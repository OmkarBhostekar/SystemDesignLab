# Read and Write Quorums

> A quorum is an acknowledgment policy over replicas; overlap can improve visibility, but only the whole protocol defines consistency.

## Why This Exists

With three replicas, waiting for all three on every read and write gives a simple freshness story but fails whenever one replica is slow or unavailable. Waiting for one is available and fast, but a read can miss a recent write. A quorum lets the system continue with a subset while seeking intersection between the replicas that acknowledged a write and those consulted for a read.

The naive slogan is `R + W > N, therefore strong consistency`. The improvement is to state the assumptions: the same replica set, a version/order rule, no stale replica returning an older version as newest, and repair/timeout behavior that respects the chosen contract. Sloppy quorums, hinted handoff, concurrent writes, clock conflict resolution, and failover can change the guarantee.

## Mental Model

For one key:

- `N` is the number of replicas that should hold the key.
- `W` is the number of replica acknowledgments required before a write response.
- `R` is the number of replica responses consulted for a read.

If `R + W > N`, any R-sized read set and W-sized write set must share at least one replica **when both sets are subsets of the same N replicas**. That intersection can reveal the write if versions are compared and the read returns the newest valid version. For `N=3`, `W=2`, `R=2`, the sets must overlap by at least one.

The arithmetic does not say which version wins, whether a concurrent write is ordered, whether an acknowledged write is durable across a region, or whether a partition can satisfy both sides. It also does not guarantee every read contacts exactly R nodes; speculative reads, local quorums, caches, and repair may alter the path.

## How It Works

A coordinator hashes a key to its replica set, sends a write to all/eligible replicas, waits for W responses, and returns success. It may record a hint for an unavailable replica. A read asks R replicas (or enough local replicas), compares versions/timestamps/vector metadata, returns a chosen value, and may repair stale replicas.

Common configurations:

| N | W | R | Typical direction | Cost |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 3 | 1 | 1 | Fast/available, weak visibility | Stale reads and repair/conflict risk |
| 3 | 2 | 2 | Quorum overlap | More latency and failure sensitivity |
| 3 | 3 | 1 | Fresh writes before success | Writes fail if any required replica is unavailable; read may trust one |
| 3 | 1 | 3 | Read freshness by sampling all | Read latency and availability depend on all |

“Majority” means more than half of a particular set; `QUORUM` may be local-datacenter, global, or an engine-specific consistency level. Read and write quorums should be aligned with failure domains: three replicas in one rack do not survive a rack failure, even if arithmetic is perfect.

## Example

An availability store has `N=3`. A write at `W=2` reaches A and B; C is partitioned. A read at `R=2` contacts A and C. The overlap A exposes the new version, assuming A responds and the protocol compares versions. A read to B and C may also see it through B. If the read instead uses `R=1`, it can contact stale C and return old data.

The improvement from `W=1,R=1` to `W=2,R=2` reduces stale-read probability/possibility under the overlap assumptions but increases latency and causes writes/reads to fail when fewer than two suitable replicas are reachable. Under a network partition, an engine may choose a sloppy quorum on non-owner nodes to preserve availability; that relaxes the simple same-replica-set intersection and requires hinted handoff/repair.

Now consider two concurrent writes to the same key. Both can receive W acknowledgments. Quorum overlap does not define a total order or business merge. Last-write-wins can discard one update based on timestamps; vector clocks or application conflict resolution may preserve alternatives. A quorum also does not make an increment safe if replicas apply non-commutative operations independently.

## Scaling Behavior

Increasing N increases copies, network traffic, repair, and storage. Raising R or W increases per-operation fan-out/latency and reduces the number of failures tolerated before an operation fails. Cross-region quorums add distance and may make a partition unavailable; local quorums trade global freshness for regional latency/availability.

At high skew, the coordinator or replica set for one key is hot even if aggregate load is balanced. Quorum read/write work also competes with hinted handoff, anti-entropy, compaction, and repair. Track p99 quorum latency, unavailable responses, conflict/repair rate, stale age, and replica-set hotness.

## Failure Modes

- **Arithmetic overclaim:** `R+W>N` is treated as linearizability or consensus.
- **Different sets:** Sloppy quorum, topology changes, or ring disagreement mean read/write sets are not the same N replicas.
- **Concurrent writes:** Overlap sees versions but does not decide business merge/order.
- **Stale cache/read path:** A cache or local replica bypasses the quorum contract.
- **Clock conflict:** Last-write-wins loses a concurrent update under clock skew or timestamp choice.
- **Repair backlog:** Hints/read repair/anti-entropy lag, extending divergence beyond the intended window.
- **Failure-domain illusion:** Majority replicas share one rack/zone and fail together.

Mitigate with an explicit consistency level per operation, failure-domain placement, version/conflict semantics, bounded hints/repair, cache policy, and tests under partitions and concurrent writes.

## Trade-offs

Higher R/W improves the chance that reads observe acknowledged writes but costs latency, network, and availability. Lower thresholds keep operations available but expose stale/divergent values and put more weight on repair. N controls redundancy and cost. Quorum choices are per workload; a session token or leader read may be simpler for read-your-writes than globally raising R.

## Alternatives

- **Leader read/write:** One ordered authority can simplify freshness and conflict handling; it is a write/failover bottleneck.
- **Synchronous replication:** Commit waits for selected copies; explicit durability/failure policy may be easier to reason about.
- **Eventual consistency plus repair:** Fast/available for counters or feeds when stale results are acceptable.
- **Consensus/linearizable register:** Needed for a strict single order/leader decision; costs coordination and is not implied by quorum arithmetic.
- **Per-session monotonic token:** Routes reads until a replica has applied a known version without making every read global.

## When to Use

Use tunable quorums when a replicated/partition-tolerant store needs explicit per-operation latency, availability, and visibility choices and its conflict/repair protocol is understood.

## When Not to Use

Do not use quorum numbers as a substitute for a transaction, uniqueness constraint, consensus, or conflict-resolution design. Avoid cross-region `R`/`W` choices that cannot meet the stated latency or partition-availability objective.

## Common Misconceptions

- **Quorum means majority always.** `R` and `W` can be any thresholds; engine terms may be local or global.
- **R plus W greater than N guarantees linearizability.** It usually provides an overlap condition under assumptions, not a total order.
- **A write is on only W replicas.** Many systems send to all eligible replicas but wait for W; verify the engine.
- **Repair is immediate.** Hints/read repair/anti-entropy can lag or be best effort.
- **More replicas remove hotspots.** A hot key can burden every replica/coordinator for that key.

## Interview Lens

### 30-Second Explanation

> For a key replicated to N nodes, W controls write acknowledgments and R controls read responses. If the read and write sets are the same N replicas and versions are compared, `R+W>N` forces overlap, improving visibility of acknowledged writes. I still specify failure domains, concurrent-write conflict resolution, sloppy/quorum behavior, repair, and latency/availability. Quorum arithmetic alone is not linearizability, consensus, or exactly-once.

### When to Introduce It

Introduce quorums when the design uses multi-primary/leaderless replication or needs a tunable read/write availability-versus-freshness policy. Start with the required operation guarantee, then select N/R/W.

### Common Follow-Ups

- What are the actual replica sets under a partition?
- How are concurrent versions merged?
- What happens when a replica is slow or missing?
- Does the operation need linearizability or only bounded staleness?
- Where do hints and repair run, and how are they monitored?

### Common Candidate Mistakes

- Writing `R+W>N` without stating assumptions.
- Confusing quorum overlap with a transaction/lock.
- Ignoring sloppy quorum and failure domains.
- Forgetting that concurrent increments need commutative/conditional handling.
- Claiming “exactly once” from an acknowledgment threshold.

## Summary

- N is replica count, R read acknowledgments, and W write acknowledgments for one operation/key.
- `R+W>N` can force set overlap under same-set/version assumptions, not a universal consistency guarantee.
- Higher thresholds cost latency/availability; lower thresholds require explicit stale/conflict/repair handling.
- Failure domains, caches, sloppy quorum, concurrent writes, and repair determine the real behavior.
