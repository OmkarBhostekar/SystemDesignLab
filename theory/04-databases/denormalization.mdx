# Denormalization and Read Models

> Denormalization spends write and repair work to make a known read path local, bounded, and cheap.

## Why This Exists

A normalized schema stores each fact once and lets queries join it. That is an excellent authority for correctness, but a high-QPS dashboard may repeatedly join orders, users, and products, or a feed may need one row per viewer and post. The naive optimization copies fields into a second table without defining which copy is true. Updates then arrive out of order, deletes leave ghosts, and a stale total is mistaken for an authoritative balance.

The improvement treats a duplicate as a projection with an owner, source version, freshness budget, update path, and rebuild plan. The trade-off is write amplification, storage, lag, duplicate handling, schema evolution, and extra observability.

## Mental Model

| Question | Required answer |
| --- | --- |
| Authority | Which store owns the fact and enforces its invariant? |
| Shape | Which exact query/result bound is the copy optimizing? |
| Update trigger | Transactional write, change stream, job, or periodic refresh? |
| Ordering/version | How does the projection reject stale/reordered updates? |
| Freshness | What max age is acceptable, and what does the user see beyond it? |
| Repair | Can it be rebuilt from authority, and at what read/write budget? |
| Delete/schema | How are tombstones, removed fields, and versions propagated? |

Denormalization is not limited to copying columns. It includes embedding, materialized views, feed fan-out, search indexes, aggregates, cache entries, and query-shaped wide-column rows. A cache may be disposable; a durable read model may need auditability and replay. State which one it is.

## How It Works

Common update patterns:

- **Synchronous dual write:** Update authority and projection in one request. Simple until one write succeeds and the other fails; a shared transaction may help only when both are in one engine.
- **Transactional intent/event:** Commit authority plus a durable intent, then asynchronously apply the projection. Avoids silent loss but exposes lag and retry/duplicate handling.
- **Change stream/CDC:** Derive changes from the authority log; ordering, retention, schema, and replay semantics must be understood.
- **Periodic rebuild/materialized refresh:** Recompute from source on a schedule; easy to reason about but freshness is bounded by refresh time.
- **Read-time join/cache:** Keep one authority and optimize selectively; lower consistency complexity but may not meet latency/scale.

Every consumer should apply a version or monotonic sequence and make updates idempotent. A projection that receives version 8 after version 9 should not regress silently. A delete needs a durable tombstone or a source-of-truth check; simply omitting the row can allow an old replay to resurrect it.

## Example

An order history page needs customer name, order status, item summary, and latest shipment state. The normalized authority has `orders`, `order_items`, `customers`, and `shipments`. At 30,000 reads/s, a join plus remote service calls misses the p99 target.

The naive fix copies current fields into `customer_order_view` during each API request. A timeout after the authority commit creates a missing view; a late shipment event overwrites a newer status; a customer rename requires a fan-out to every historical order.

The improved design keeps order/payment state authoritative, records a versioned change intent in the local write path, and applies an idempotent projection keyed by `(customer_id, order_id)`. The projection stores an order snapshot and source versions. A rebuild reads authority in pages, writes a new generation, verifies counts/checksums, and swaps the reader to the new generation. Customer names can be copied with an explicit policy: historical orders may retain the name at purchase time, while a current profile name may be a separate field.

The new trade-off is visible: a newly shipped order may show `shipment_pending` for seconds, and the UI/metrics must expose projection age. If the product requires immediate status, route that one read to the authority or wait for the projection version; do not silently call an eventually consistent view “real time.”

## Scaling Behavior

Denormalization moves work from reads to writes. A one-to-many fan-out can make a single source update touch millions of rows; a hot customer or celebrity post can dominate. Async workers need queue capacity, retry budgets, ordering keys, and replay throughput. Rebuilding a projection can compete with foreground reads and authority I/O.

At high data volume, store only fields required by the query and use bounded buckets/generations. Track freshness by source-to-projection version/time, not merely worker health. Multi-region projections may be local and lagging; document whether a region can serve stale reads during a partition.

## Failure Modes

- **Dual-write split:** Authority commits but projection fails or vice versa.
- **Reordered update:** Older event overwrites newer state.
- **Duplicate event:** The same change is applied twice and increments/side effects duplicate.
- **Lost delete:** A deleted row remains or is resurrected by replay.
- **Fan-out storm:** One source update writes millions of copies and starves normal work.
- **Projection drift:** A bug or dropped event leaves a view silently incorrect.
- **Schema mismatch:** Producer changes fields before all projection consumers understand the version.
- **Unsafe rebuild:** A backfill and live updates race, creating gaps or regressions at cutover.

Mitigate with durable intents/CDC, idempotent version checks, tombstones, bounded fan-out, lag/drift metrics, schema compatibility, replay, shadow comparison, and generation-based cutover.

## Trade-offs

Denormalization can make critical reads fast, local, and predictable, reduce joins/fan-out, and isolate a read workload. It costs extra storage and write work, stale/duplicate copies, backfill/rebuild complexity, and a larger correctness surface. Synchronous copies reduce lag but couple request latency/failure; async copies improve decoupling but require an honest freshness contract.

## Alternatives

- **Composite/covering index:** Prefer when one query needs a better local access path without duplicating a full view.
- **Materialized view:** Prefer for a database-managed aggregate/refresh policy; verify refresh locking and freshness.
- **Cache:** Prefer for disposable repeated hot reads; still define invalidation and origin fallback.
- **Read-time join:** Prefer at modest scale or when freshness is critical and latency budget permits.
- **Search/analytics projection:** Prefer when the query semantics differ substantially; keep rebuildability and authority clear.

## When to Use

Use denormalization after identifying a measured read bottleneck or a query shape that cannot be served locally enough. Name the source, fields copied, update trigger/order, freshness, invalidation/delete, and rebuild plan.

## When Not to Use

Do not duplicate a mutable field just to avoid one cheap join, and do not use a projection as a correctness authority without a deliberate ownership transfer. Avoid unbounded fan-out when a query-time or bucketed alternative meets the target.

## Common Misconceptions

- **Denormalization means inconsistent data is acceptable.** The freshness/consistency contract still needs to be explicit.
- **A dual write is atomic because it is in one function.** A crash can split the effects.
- **Events arrive once and in order.** Delivery can duplicate, delay, reorder, or lose data without a durable protocol.
- **Materialized views never need repair.** Bugs, missed updates, and schema changes require replay/rebuild.
- **Every source update should fan out immediately.** Batch/coalesce, pull, or bounded projection strategies may be safer.

## Interview Lens

### 30-Second Explanation

> I denormalize a measured, bounded read path while keeping one authority for each fact. The projection update is synchronous only when latency/freshness and failure coupling justify it; otherwise a durable intent/CDC path applies versioned, idempotent updates. I state lag, duplicate/reorder/delete behavior, fan-out cost, schema compatibility, and how a new generation is rebuilt and cut over.

### When to Introduce It

Introduce it when joins/fan-out or a query-shaped store is the first measured bottleneck and stale-read budget is explicit.

### Common Follow-Ups

- What is the source of truth?
- How do you prevent an old event from overwriting a new value?
- What does a delete look like?
- How is the projection rebuilt after a bug?
- What happens to a celebrity update with millions of readers?

### Common Candidate Mistakes

- Writing two stores without an ordering/idempotency protocol.
- Calling a projection “strongly consistent” because it is updated asynchronously.
- Ignoring schema evolution and historical versus current copied fields.
- Forgetting rebuild, drift detection, and cutover safety.
- Treating fan-out writes as free at scale.

## Summary

- Denormalization is an intentional read/write trade, not an accidental duplicate.
- Keep authority, versions, freshness, deletes, and rebuild behavior explicit.
- Async projections need idempotency, ordering, lag metrics, replay, and drift repair.
- A narrow index or cache may solve the bottleneck with less consistency machinery.
