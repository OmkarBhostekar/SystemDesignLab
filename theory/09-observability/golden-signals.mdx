# Golden Signals

> The four golden signals are a compact starting lens: measure what users wait for, send, fail to do, and what resource is running out.

## Why This Exists

An operations team can collect thousands of host, runtime, and dependency metrics and still miss a customer-visible outage. CPU may be normal while a queue is full; request volume may be high while every request returns a cached error; average latency may look healthy while the slowest 1% times out. The naive dashboard lists whatever a platform exports, not what decisions the service needs to make.

Golden signals compress the first operational question into four dimensions: latency, traffic, errors, and saturation. They are not a complete monitoring system or a universal SLO template. They help an operator detect the symptom, size the event, and choose whether to shed work, add capacity, roll back, or investigate a dependency.

## Mental Model

```text
traffic → work admitted → latency experienced → errors observed
                └──────── saturation / queueing ────────┘
```

- **Latency:** time experienced by the caller, usually distributions and percentiles, not only a mean.
- **Traffic:** demand entering a boundary, such as requests/s, messages/s, bytes/s, or active connections.
- **Errors:** requests or operations that did not meet the contract, including explicit failures, timeouts, rejected work, and sometimes invalid business outcomes.
- **Saturation:** how close a limiting resource is to exhaustion—CPU, connections, queue capacity, memory, rate limit, worker slots, or dependency budget.

The signal boundary matters. A gateway's 200 response may represent an asynchronous acceptance, while the user's actual business completion fails later. Define whether “error” means HTTP failure, eventual job failure, or both, and show each separately.

## How It Works

At the user-facing edge, count requests by route template, method, status class, and outcome. Record latency as a histogram or suitable distribution so p50, p95, and p99 can be computed. For traffic that fans out, distinguish incoming demand from downstream call rate; retries are additional traffic even when user request rate is unchanged.

Define an error policy. A timeout, rejected queue admission, and dependency failure often count as errors if the user did not get the promised result. A 404 for a missing resource may be a valid outcome rather than an error. Business failure (no inventory) may deserve its own outcome even if the HTTP status is 200.

Measure saturation at the bottleneck: queue depth and age, worker utilization, DB connection pool wait, memory pressure, rate-limit tokens, or dependency concurrency. CPU alone is a poor proxy for a blocked IO service. Record capacity and headroom so an operator can predict time to exhaustion.

Use the signals as a loop:

1. Confirm user-impact latency/error change.
2. Compare traffic to normal and identify whether demand or behavior changed.
3. Locate saturation or queue growth at the first constrained hop.
4. Apply the least risky mitigation: stop a rollout, shed optional work, increase capacity, or isolate a dependency.
5. Verify recovery and watch for retry amplification or backlog debt.

## Example

An image-processing API receives 500 requests/s, with p99 latency rising from 800 ms to 8 s and 5xx errors at 3%. CPU is only 55%, but the worker queue is 95% full and oldest job age grows. Traffic is normal; saturation is in worker slots and queue capacity. The immediate action is to reject or defer low-priority jobs with a retry-after/status endpoint, then add workers or reduce per-image cost. Adding web servers alone cannot drain the bottleneck.

For an asynchronous email service, user-facing request latency may remain 50 ms while queue age grows from 1 minute to 45 minutes. The golden signal is not only HTTP latency: message traffic, delivery failure rate, oldest queue age, and worker saturation describe the promised completion.

## Visualization We Eventually Want

Build a workload simulator with request rate, service capacity, queue size, dependency latency, failure rate, and retry toggles. Plot p50/p95/p99 latency, incoming vs retry traffic, errors by contract, queue age/depth, and resource saturation. Presets should include a CPU-bound service, IO-bound connection-pool exhaustion, and an async worker backlog. Let the learner apply rate limiting, load shedding, rollback, or capacity and observe which signals move first.

## Scaling Behavior

As traffic grows, aggregate metrics by bounded dimensions and retain tenant/route breakdowns only where they support a decision. Percentiles should be computed from histograms or mergeable distributions; averaging per-instance p99 values can misrepresent the fleet. In a sharded or multi-region service, report fleet and worst-partition views.

Saturation is often nonlinear: queueing latency can stay low until utilization approaches capacity, then increase sharply. A worker can be “50% CPU” but saturated on a downstream pool. For async work, queue age and lag are often more predictive than depth because a few huge jobs can hide old work.

## Failure Modes

- **Average-only latency:** a small timeout population disappears in the mean.
- **Wrong error denominator:** counting only 5xx hides client timeouts, rejected work, or eventual job failures.
- **CPU as saturation:** IO, connection, memory, and queue limits exhaust first.
- **Traffic blind spot:** retries and fan-out inflate dependency load without increasing user request count.
- **Unbounded labels:** per-user or raw-path dimensions make dashboards and alerts unusable.
- **Delayed completion ignored:** an async API reports success while its queue silently misses its completion objective.
- **Metric pipeline failure:** a healthy-looking dashboard reflects stale or partial telemetry.

## Trade-offs

Golden signals are easy to communicate and map to action, but they omit domain-specific correctness, cost, and security signals. More dimensions aid diagnosis but increase cardinality and operator cognitive load. Aggressive alert thresholds catch short blips but create noise; longer windows reduce pages but delay response. A single fleet aggregate hides hotspots; per-tenant or per-region views cost more but expose fairness and blast radius.

## Alternatives

- **USE method (utilization, saturation, errors):** useful for resource-centric infrastructure diagnosis; pair with user latency and traffic.
- **RED method (rate, errors, duration):** convenient for request-driven services; add queue and resource saturation for async or capacity-limited systems.
- **Business KPIs:** necessary for correctness (orders completed, money settled), but should not replace technical symptoms.
- **Full SLO dashboard:** best once user journeys and objectives are defined; golden signals are the first compact view.

## When to Use

Use golden signals on every user-facing service, worker pool, and dependency boundary. Define each signal in terms of the contract and bottleneck, then connect it to a runbook action.

## When Not to Use

Do not treat the four names as a checklist that proves reliability. A batch pipeline may need freshness and correctness; a payment system needs settlement state; a security system needs detection latency. Add domain indicators where they change decisions.

## Common Misconceptions

- **“Traffic is always user requests.”** It can be messages, bytes, fan-out calls, retries, or active streams.
- **“An error is every non-2xx response.”** Valid business outcomes and client errors need an explicit contract.
- **“Saturation means CPU > 80%.”** Any bounded resource can saturate.
- **“Golden signals identify root cause.”** They expose symptoms and likely bottlenecks; traces/logs establish causal evidence.
- **“A queue depth of zero means healthy.”** A stuck consumer may have no visible depth while completed work is late.

## Interview Lens

### 30-Second Explanation

> I start with user-boundary latency percentiles, demand rate, contract-defined errors, and the first constrained resource. I include retries and fan-out in traffic, measure queue age for async work, and avoid CPU-only saturation proxies. The signals tell me whether to roll back, shed optional work, add capacity, or trace a dependency; they are symptoms, not a complete root-cause model.

### When to Introduce It

Introduce golden signals as soon as a service has a user or downstream contract, especially before selecting autoscaling, alert thresholds, or failure mitigation.

### Common Follow-Ups

- Which failures count in your error rate?
- Why is p99 more useful than mean latency here?
- How do you measure saturation for a queue consumer?
- What if traffic is normal but errors spike?

### Common Candidate Mistakes

- Showing only CPU, memory, and request count.
- Alerting on a single instance's noisy percentile.
- Ignoring retries as extra traffic.
- Forgetting business completion after asynchronous acceptance.

## Summary

Latency, traffic, errors, and saturation create a small, actionable first view. Define them at the right contract boundary, use distributions and bounded dimensions, include async age and retry load, and pair symptoms with traces, logs, and business outcomes.

## Quiz Seeds

1. Why can p99 latency rise while average latency barely changes?
2. Name two forms of saturation that are not CPU utilization.
3. Should a valid “item not found” response count as a server error? What contract decides?
4. Why must retries be included in downstream traffic?
5. Which signal reveals an async worker is late even when HTTP request latency is low?
6. A queue is 95% full but CPU is 40%. What bottleneck is plausible, and what action follows?
7. Why are fleet-wide percentile averages unsafe for tail diagnosis?
