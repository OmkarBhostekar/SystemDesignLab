# CAP Theorem

> Under a network partition, a replicated service cannot both guarantee linearizable consistency and guarantee an eligible response from every non-failing side; partition tolerance is the operating assumption that makes the choice relevant.

## Why This Exists

Replicas let a service survive node and network failures, but a partition can split replicas that cannot communicate. If both sides accept conflicting writes, a later read may violate a single, real-time order. If one side refuses to answer or write until coordination is restored, it preserves that order but sacrifices availability for that operation.

The naive explanation is “pick any two of consistency, availability, and partition tolerance forever.” That phrasing hides the actual event: a partition. In a real distributed system, partitions and delayed messages are possible; designers choose whether the operation fails closed (CP-like) or continues with weaker semantics (AP-like) during that event.

## Mental Model

CAP uses precise terms:

- **Consistency (C):** in the theorem's usual form, linearizability: each operation appears to take effect atomically at one point between invocation and response, respecting real-time order.
- **Availability (A):** every request to a non-failing node eventually receives a response, even when the partition prevents coordination. This is not the same as a service's uptime SLO.
- **Partition tolerance (P):** the system continues to operate under arbitrary message loss or delay that divides replicas into communicating groups. P is an assumption about the network, not a feature you can purchase away.

When a partition occurs, preserving both C and A is impossible for operations that touch the split replicated state. A service can preserve consistency by refusing or delaying one side, or preserve response availability by allowing divergent state and resolving it later.

## How It Works

Imagine replicas A and B storing one account balance. A partition prevents messages between them.

~~~text
before:        A  ⇄  B
partition:     A  ║  B
client writes  +10     +20
~~~

### CP behavior

A CP-like design chooses a single quorum, leader, or owner that can safely decide the order. The minority side rejects or times out writes; some reads may also fail or return only data that meets the consistency rule. Once communication returns, replicas catch up without exposing conflicting committed values.

### AP behavior

An AP-like design lets both sides answer with local state. Writes may diverge, and reads may be stale or conflict-bearing. Repair, versioning, deterministic merge, or application reconciliation resolves the divergence. “Available” does not mean “always correct” or “never stale.”

These are behavior choices for a data item and operation. A service can use CP-like inventory writes and AP-like view counters, or offer multiple read/write consistency modes. The theorem does not classify every component with one permanent label.

## Example

Compare a ticket inventory reservation with social “like” counts.

### Naive → failure → improvement

1. **Naive:** allow both sides of a partition to decrement the last ticket because “the service must stay available.”
2. **Failure:** two buyers receive confirmed reservations for one item; reconciliation cannot create a second ticket.
3. **Improvement:** make inventory reservation CP-like: require the owner/quorum, reject or queue writes when it cannot be reached, and expose a retryable result. Let like counts use AP-like increments with later merge.
4. **New trade-off:** inventory loses write availability for a partitioned side, while likes may be temporarily inconsistent or overcounted until repair.

The product requirement—not the database brand—determines where the trade-off belongs. A “checkout page available” metric cannot justify returning two successful reservations.

- animate replicas, message delay, stale reads, conflicts, merge, and repair;
- display linearizability violations, acknowledged operations, user-visible availability, and final convergence;
- include presets for inventory, profile edits, and like counters.

The visualization must label the partition event and explain that CP/AP behavior is chosen during the partition, not a slogan for all operating conditions.

## Scaling Behavior

More replicas and regions increase the chance that a partition matters and increase coordination and repair paths. Quorum sizes, leader placement, traffic routing, and replication lag affect normal latency as well as partition behavior. A cross-region synchronous quorum may preserve stronger semantics at the cost of inter-region latency and write availability.

Partition handling can be per key or shard. A local read may remain available while a cross-shard transaction cannot. A system must also preserve capacity after a partition heals; replaying or merging a large backlog can overload the recovered replicas.

## Failure Modes

- **Split-brain writes:** two sides commit incompatible values.
- **Misnamed availability:** a fast stale or conflicting response is counted as success for a critical invariant.
- **Quorum loss:** a CP path rejects operations even though one replica is reachable.
- **Conflict explosion:** AP repair cannot merge arbitrary application state safely.
- **Stale reads:** a read is served from a replica that has not received the latest write.
- **Healing storm:** replay and reconciliation overload the network or storage.
- **Over-broad classification:** calling an entire database CP or AP hides operation-specific behavior.

Mitigate with explicit consistency contracts, ownership/fencing, versioning, conflict-free data types where appropriate, bounded queues, repair monitoring, and a per-operation failure policy.

## Trade-offs

CP-like behavior protects invariants and simplifies reads at the cost of partition-time availability and possibly latency. AP-like behavior preserves local responses and can reduce coordination cost at the cost of stale or conflicting state, merge logic, and user-visible uncertainty.

Partition tolerance is not optional in a system that relies on an unreliable network; “choosing CA” usually means assuming no partition or using one logical failure domain. Even a single-region system must decide how to behave when replicas cannot communicate.

CAP says little about normal-operation latency, durability, transaction scope, or the cost of coordination outside a partition. Those are separate trade-offs; PACELC is one later framing, not a replacement for stating the partition behavior.

## Alternatives

- **Single writer with replicas:** reduce write conflicts while accepting failover or partition-time unavailability.
- **Quorum/leader protocol:** coordinate enough replicas for a safe decision; choose quorum and failure behavior explicitly.
- **AP with mergeable state:** use counters, sets, or CRDT-like structures when concurrent updates have a safe merge.
- **Partition-local queue:** accept intent durably and apply it after ownership returns; expose pending state.
- **Centralized source of truth:** use when the invariant is more valuable than multi-region write availability and the latency budget allows.

## When to Use

Use CAP reasoning when a design has replicated state, multiple regions, active-active writes, leader/quorum decisions, or a requirement for both high availability and strong consistency. Ask what should happen when communication fails.

## When Not to Use

Do not invoke CAP for a single-process cache or as a generic explanation for every database trade-off. Do not say “eventual consistency solves CAP” without explaining conflicts, repair, and the product invariant. Do not promise a CP design can always serve both sides of a partition.

## Common Misconceptions

- **“CAP means choose any two forever.”** The trade-off appears when a partition occurs.
- **“P is optional.”** In a distributed network, message delay and loss are an operating reality; P is a model assumption.
- **“A means uptime.”** CAP availability is a response guarantee for requests to non-failing nodes.
- **“C means ACID consistency.”** CAP's C is usually linearizability for replicated operations, not all ACID properties.
- **“AP means incorrect forever.”** It can converge with repair, but the application must handle stale/conflicting state.
- **“A database is simply CP or AP.”** Behavior can vary by operation, quorum, topology, and configuration.

## Interview Lens

### 30-Second Explanation

> CAP is about a replicated system during a network partition. If replicas cannot communicate, a design either preserves linearizable consistency by rejecting or delaying some operations, or keeps responding on both sides and accepts stale or conflicting state that must be repaired. I would classify each business operation, explain its partition behavior, and avoid treating CAP as a permanent “pick two” label or as a statement about ACID.

### When to Introduce It

Introduce CAP after replication and fault domains, when the prompt asks for multi-region writes, always-on behavior, or strong consistency. Draw the partition before naming CP or AP.

### Common Follow-Ups

- What exactly is the partition and which nodes can still communicate?
- Which operations must reject during the partition?
- What does a client observe on each side?
- How are AP conflicts merged or surfaced?
- What happens while the partition heals?

### Common Candidate Mistakes

- Saying “choose two” without showing the partition.
- Calling a timeout “available.”
- Treating CAP C as ACID consistency.
- Choosing AP for non-mergeable inventory or payment state.
- Ignoring repair, stale reads, and conflict observability.

## Summary

- CAP concerns consistency and response availability under a network partition.
- Partition tolerance is an assumption about messages; the choice is whether to sacrifice C or A when the partition occurs.
- CP-like paths reject or delay unsafe operations; AP-like paths accept local progress and reconcile later.
- Apply the trade-off per operation and invariant, not as a permanent database label.
- CAP does not replace reasoning about latency, durability, recovery, or normal-operation performance.
