# Leader Election

> Leader election creates a temporary single writer or coordinator; it is safe only when followers can recognize stale leadership and the system defines what happens during uncertainty.

## Why This Exists

Suppose ten workers poll a due-jobs table. With no coordination, all ten can claim the same job, or each can race on a global schedule. The naive fix is to configure one “primary” worker manually. That becomes a single point of failure: when it dies, the schedule stops until a human changes configuration.

Leader election lets multiple nodes be ready while one node performs a role. The role may be “assign partitions,” “advance a scheduler,” or “accept writes for a shard.” Election is not a magic availability feature: choosing a leader takes time, may be impossible during a partition, and does not by itself replicate the leader's state.

## Mental Model

Think of a group of candidates and one **term/epoch** at a time:

```text
normal:   leader A ──heartbeats──> followers B C D
failure:  B C D stop hearing A
election: candidates ask for votes in a newer term
result:   one candidate obtains the required quorum
handoff:  new leader fences or supersedes old work
```

The most important question is not “which node wins?” but “under what evidence may a node act as leader?” A timeout is a suspicion, not proof of death. If a partition leaves two groups each believing the other is gone, a quorum rule or fencing mechanism must prevent two writers from committing conflicting work.

## How It Works

A simplified election uses:

1. A current leader sends periodic heartbeats.
2. A follower that misses a randomized election timeout becomes a candidate and increments an epoch/term.
3. It requests votes from peers. A peer votes at most once per term and rejects stale terms.
4. The candidate acts as leader after a quorum (for example, 3 of 5) grants votes.
5. The leader sends heartbeats and includes the term in commands or log entries.
6. A node that observes a higher term steps down; a leader that loses contact with a quorum must stop making authoritative progress.

Randomized timeouts reduce simultaneous candidacies, while quorum voting prevents two leaders from both having a majority in a five-node group. The exact protocol matters. ZooKeeper's recipe uses ephemeral sequential znodes and watches only the next lower candidate to avoid a notification herd. Raft combines election with a replicated log and has stronger, explicit safety rules; a home-grown “heartbeat plus majority” is not automatically Raft.

Election state should be durable or recoverable. If a node restarts and forgets the current term, it can accidentally accept stale messages. A leader also needs a handoff contract: stop accepting new work, finish or cancel in-flight work, and publish ownership/fencing information before another node takes over.

## Example

An email scheduler has five identical workers and one queue of due notifications. Every worker can send email, but only one should advance a global partition assignment. Worker A is leader in term 12. A network issue cuts A off from B, C, and D while A can still reach an external provider.

If the election rule is “whoever times out becomes leader,” A and B can both send. With a majority quorum, B must obtain three votes in term 13. A cannot obtain a majority and must stop assigning new jobs once it learns it lacks quorum. Jobs already sent need idempotency/provider deduplication because election does not rewind an external side effect.

When B wins, it writes a term-13 assignment and workers reject assignments from term 12. If the store cannot fence term-12 work, add a monotonically increasing fencing token to each claim and make the queue/database reject stale tokens.

## Visualization We Eventually Want

Show five nodes with state (`follower`, `candidate`, `leader`), current term, election deadlines, heartbeat arrows, and vote responses. Controls should step time, pause one node, partition links, delay messages, change quorum size, and choose deterministic or randomized timeouts. A **“leader loses quorum”** preset should show a stale leader ceasing authoritative work, a new candidate gathering votes, and a job claim rejected because its term/token is old. The visualization must label the educational simplifications; it should not imply that network timeout proves process death.

## Scaling Behavior

Election traffic is roughly proportional to members and heartbeat frequency; large groups increase vote and failure-detection work. A coordinator group is usually kept small, while data-plane workers scale separately. More regions increase round-trip time and the probability of a partition, so election timeout and quorum placement are tied to latency and failure domains.

An odd number of voters improves fault tolerance: a 3-voter group tolerates one unavailable voter; a 5-voter group tolerates two, assuming the remaining voters can communicate. Adding observers/non-voters can improve read locality without changing the quorum, but the protocol must distinguish them from voters.

## Failure Modes

- **Split brain:** two leaders act because the protocol lacks quorum/fencing, or because each side can reach a different majority due to misconfiguration.
- **False suspicion:** a long GC pause or slow network triggers election even though the old leader is alive.
- **Election storm:** synchronized timeouts or an overloaded coordinator cause repeated candidacies and no stable leader.
- **Stale leader:** a paused leader resumes and writes after a new term; term checks or fencing must reject it.
- **No quorum:** the system stays available only for reads or non-authoritative work, depending on the design; forcing progress can violate consistency.
- **Unsafe handoff:** the new leader repeats external side effects because the old leader's in-flight result was unknown.

## Trade-offs

Leader-based coordination simplifies per-key ordering and reduces write conflicts, but introduces leader load, failover delay, and a quorum dependency. Randomized timeouts improve convergence but make detection less deterministic. A lease can lower steady-state heartbeat traffic but relies on bounded timing and fencing; a consensus-backed term is stronger but costs round trips and durable coordination.

## Alternatives

- **Stateless work stealing:** prefer when duplicate execution is cheap and idempotent; no global leader is required.
- **Partitioned ownership:** prefer when each key has an independent owner and a central leader would bottleneck the workload.
- **External coordinator:** ZooKeeper/etcd/consensus service can supply membership and election; it becomes a dependency with its own quorum and operations.
- **Manual or DNS failover:** acceptable for rare human-controlled recovery, not for per-second scheduling decisions.

## When to Use

Use election when exactly one active coordinator simplifies correctness: partition assignment, scheduler tick ownership, metadata mutation, or a single-writer shard. Define the quorum, failure detector, epoch, handoff, and side-effect deduplication before introducing the leader box.

## When Not to Use

Do not elect a leader just because the architecture has multiple services. Avoid one global leader for independent keys when partitioned ownership scales better. Do not use a leader alone to guarantee durable history; pair it with replicated state or an authoritative store.

## Common Misconceptions

- **“The first node that times out is leader.”** It must win the protocol's vote/quorum rules.
- **“A heartbeat proves the leader is alive everywhere.”** It proves only an observed communication path.
- **“Leader election is consensus.”** Election chooses a coordinator; consensus also agrees on a safe replicated history/state.
- **“A new leader cancels old side effects.”** External calls may already have succeeded; idempotency and reconciliation remain necessary.
- **“No quorum means choose the freshest node.”** Without a safe quorum/term rule, freshness may be unknown under partition.

## Interview Lens

### 30-Second Explanation

> I use leader election when one active owner simplifies a shared task. Followers detect a missed heartbeat, candidates request votes in a newer term, and a quorum makes leadership unambiguous. Election is not proof of failure and not the same as consensus, so the design must stop a leader that loses quorum, fence stale terms, and reconcile in-flight external work.

### When to Introduce It

Introduce election after identifying a single-writer or singleton-scheduler requirement and before drawing a “primary” box. Ask what failure detector, quorum, term, and handoff semantics make that box safe.

### Common Follow-Ups

- Why do you need an odd number of voters?
- What happens during a region partition?
- How does a restarted old leader avoid writing stale state?
- What is stored durably, and how does a new leader rebuild ownership?

### Common Candidate Mistakes

- Confusing a health check with quorum leadership.
- Ignoring the old leader's delayed messages.
- Claiming failover is instantaneous and lossless.
- Making every worker watch one node and causing a thundering herd.

## Summary

Election is a temporary authority decision under imperfect failure detection. Use terms/epochs, quorum rules, randomized timeouts, safe handoff, and fencing for stale leaders. Pair it with replicated state or a durable source of truth when the role controls data, and keep the coordination group small enough to converge predictably.

## Quiz Seeds

1. Why can two nodes both believe they are leader after a network partition if no quorum rule exists?
2. What does a term/epoch protect against when an old leader resumes?
3. Why are randomized election timeouts useful?
4. What is the difference between a leader election result and a committed replicated log entry?
5. A five-node group loses two nodes. Can it elect a leader? What changes if it loses three?
6. Why must a scheduler still deduplicate a job after a successful election handoff?
7. When might partitioned ownership be better than one global leader?
