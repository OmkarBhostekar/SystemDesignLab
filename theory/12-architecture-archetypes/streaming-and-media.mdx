# Streaming and Media Systems

> Media architecture is sustained byte delivery plus control metadata: store and transform large objects, then distribute cacheable segments close to viewers while adapting to network conditions.

## Why This Exists

Serving a 2 GB video directly from one application server works for a demo and fails when 10,000 viewers request it: origin bandwidth, connections, and regional latency multiply. A live stream adds continuously arriving data, startup latency, and a moving edge. Interactive voice/video has a different shape again: low latency and jitter matter more than cacheability.

The improvement separates ingest, metadata/manifests, media objects/segments, transcoding, and delivery. On-demand media is segmented and cached through a CDN; live media produces rolling segments and manifests; interactive media may use a real-time transport rather than HTTP object delivery. The new trade-offs are storage/egress cost, cache invalidation, encoding delay, bitrate ladders, regional failover, and content protection.

## Mental Model

```text
upload/live ingest ─> durable source ─> transcode/package ─> segments + manifest
                                                                    │
viewer ─ manifest ─> CDN edge ─ cache miss ─> origin/object store ──┘
```

Separate payload and control paths. The manifest tells a client which representations/segments exist; segments carry bytes and should usually be immutable/versioned. The client measures throughput/buffer and chooses a bitrate. A CDN serves repeated segment requests, while origin capacity should not scale linearly with viewers.

## How It Works

For on-demand video, upload a source object, transcode into a bitrate/resolution ladder, segment it, generate a manifest, and publish metadata atomically enough that clients never see a manifest pointing to missing segments. Use immutable segment names and versioned manifests so CDN caches remain safe.

For live, ingest creates segments continuously. A sliding manifest exposes the recent window; segment availability and encoder/origin delay define end-to-end latency. CDN caching of the newest segment is shorter and more sensitive to origin bursts. Backpressure, dropped frames, or quality reduction may preserve continuity.

A CDN should authenticate access at the manifest/token layer and scope signed URLs/cookies; do not expose an unrestricted origin. Origin shield/peer cache and multi-region replicas reduce thundering misses. DRM/encryption/license services are separate control dependencies; encrypting bytes does not itself authorize playback.

## Example

A video platform has 1 million daily viewers, a 5 Mb/s average stream, and a live launch with 100,000 concurrent viewers. Direct origin serving would require roughly 500 Gb/s at the launch (100,000 × 5 Mb/s), before overhead. A CDN with 95% segment cache hit ratio leaves about 5,000 origin-equivalent streams during steady state, though a new live segment can cause a synchronized miss.

The architecture stores originals in object storage, transcodes asynchronously, publishes HLS/DASH-like manifests and immutable segments, and uses a CDN. The client adapts bitrate from throughput/buffer. A regional origin failure shifts to replicated objects/CDN; a manifest service remains a smaller control-plane dependency. If the live encoder falls behind, the system lowers rendition, drops nonessential frames, or increases latency according to product policy.

The new trade-off is startup/transcode delay, egress cost, CDN invalidation/versioning, and a larger operational pipeline. Interactive video calls should not copy this architecture wholesale: their latency/jitter and per-session routing requirements are different.

## Scaling Behavior

Sustained bandwidth dominates: viewers × bitrate × concurrency, plus protocol/encoding overhead. Storage includes originals, renditions, segments, replicas, and retention. Segment duration trades request count/startup latency against cacheability and live latency. Smaller segments reduce latency but increase request/origin metadata and CDN churn.

CDN hit ratio and regional demand determine origin load. A popular release can cause a cache miss storm at publication; prewarm known objects or use origin shield. Live newest segments have lower reuse than old on-demand segments. Transcoding is CPU/GPU-heavy and should be queued with backpressure; output explosion is proportional to renditions × duration.

## Failure Modes

- **Origin thundering herd:** many edges miss a new segment together.
- **Missing segment/manifest race:** client receives control metadata before bytes are ready.
- **Encoder/transcoder lag:** live latency grows or renditions diverge.
- **CDN/region outage:** origin traffic and latency surge during failover.
- **Slow viewer:** buffer drains, causing rebuffer or excessive server state.
- **Bitrate ladder mismatch:** client chooses a rendition the network cannot sustain.
- **Signed URL/DRM failure:** playback fails despite available bytes.
- **Storage lifecycle mistake:** a manifest points to expired segments.
- **Egress cost spike:** a viral object bypasses cache or traffic shifts regions.

Use immutable/versioned objects, manifest readiness checks, cache/origin shielding, prewarming, bounded transcoder queues, adaptive bitrate, regional replicas, signed token scopes, lifecycle validation, and cost telemetry.

## Trade-offs

CDN caching lowers origin cost/latency but adds invalidation and provider dependence. Smaller segments lower live latency but raise request and cache overhead. More renditions improve adaptation but multiply transcode/storage/egress. Multi-region improves failover but costs replication and control-plane complexity. HTTP segment delivery is cache-friendly; interactive transports trade cacheability for latency/jitter.

## Alternatives

- **Direct object download:** choose for low concurrency or non-streaming files.
- **Progressive HTTP download:** choose for simple on-demand media with modest adaptation needs.
- **HLS/DASH-style adaptive segments:** choose for broad internet delivery and CDN caching.
- **WebRTC/interactive media:** choose for sub-second interactive calls/games.
- **Single-region origin + CDN:** choose when RTO/latency/business geography do not justify multi-region storage.
- **Managed media pipeline:** choose when encoding/packaging complexity exceeds product differentiation, while retaining workload estimates.

## When to Use

Use this archetype when payloads are large or continuous and delivery bandwidth/latency dominate. Classify on-demand, live, upload/transcode, and interactive paths separately.

## When Not to Use

Do not use a CDN segment design for transactional APIs or interactive calls that need sub-second state exchange. Do not assume “media” means one bitrate, one region, or one retention window.

## Common Misconceptions

- **“CDN solves origin scale completely.”** Cache misses, new live segments, and regional failure still hit origin.
- **“More bitrate is always better.”** It raises bandwidth/rebuffer risk on constrained clients.
- **“A manifest is the media.”** It is control metadata pointing to versioned segments.
- **“Streaming and video calls are the same.”** On-demand/live segment delivery differs from interactive low-latency sessions.
- **“Signed URLs provide DRM.”** They limit capability; encryption/license policy is separate.

## Interview Lens

### 30-Second Explanation

> I separate ingest, metadata/manifests, transcoding, immutable media segments, and delivery. On-demand segments are CDN-cacheable; live manifests/segments use a rolling window and bounded latency; interactive media may need a different transport. I estimate viewers × bitrate, origin/cache hit ratio, segment/request overhead, encoding/storage/egress cost, and regional failover. Adaptive bitrate, origin shielding, signed access, lifecycle checks, and backpressure handle real-world failures.

### When to Introduce It

Introduce it after clarifying media type, concurrency, bitrate, startup/live-latency target, retention, geography, and whether interaction is required.

### Common Follow-Ups

- What is cached and what is immutable?
- How do you handle a viral launch/cache miss storm?
- How does a client choose bitrate?
- What changes for live versus on-demand?
- How does regional origin failure affect cost and latency?

### Common Candidate Mistakes

They draw a CDN without estimating sustained bandwidth, omit packaging/transcode/backpressure, conflate WebRTC with on-demand HTTP, or ignore segment/manifest races and egress cost.

## Summary

Media systems are bandwidth pipelines with a small control plane. Segment/version payloads, cache them near viewers, adapt to network conditions, and plan ingest/transcode/storage/egress/failover separately. Change the design when the workload shifts from replay to live or interactive.
